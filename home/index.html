The problem

Farm decisions need many datasets. Tools look at one at a time.

In Tajikistan and northern Afghanistan, summer heat passes 40 °C, irrigation water is shared and limited, and years of continuous wheat or cotton draw down soil nitrogen. Climate, water, soil and nutrition are usually analysed in separate tools. A farmer choosing next year's crop has to combine them alone.

Water

How much irrigation does each rotation need in a normal year, a dry year, a hotter future?

Soil

Which rotation rebuilds nitrogen with legumes, and which one runs it down and needs more fertiliser?

Food and income

How many people can one hectare feed, and where does the familiar rotation still earn more in year one?

How it works

From a field outline to three explained rotations

The equations produce the numbers; the interface only explains them. The method is open source and covered by unit tests, including FAO-56 worked examples.

01 / PICK

Pick a field

Fly to it on a 3D globe and outline the field, or start from one of four preset regions.

02 / READ

Read NASA data

Daily NASA POWER weather (2001–2024), MODIS crop greenness, root-zone soil wetness and NASADEM terrain.

03 / COMPUTE

Run open equations

FAO-56 Penman–Monteith evapotranspiration, a daily soil-water balance, FAO-33 yield response and a nitrogen balance.

04 / DECIDE

Compare and decide

Three rotations, each with why it is suggested, NASA evidence, benefits, data gaps and local risks for the farmer to review.

What only the combination shows

Where datasets disagree, there is a question worth asking

Fihrist puts weather, soil water and satellite greenness on one monthly grid and compares each month with the field's own 24-year history. It flags months where crops were much less green than usual although heat, rain and soil water were normal. The cause lies outside the weather record.

First result: all three Central Asian presets show this in March–April 2012; Kansas does not. The winter before was unusually cold at all three sites. Winter-kill or late green-up would fit, but this is a hypothesis to confirm with farmers, not a finding. The three sites share one regional winter.

About

Why "Fihrist"

Fihrist means "index" in Persian and Arabic. In the 10th century, Ibn al-Nadim's Kitab al-Fihrist catalogued the knowledge of his time across disciplines. Scholars of the same tradition, such as Al-Biruni and Ibn Sina, worked across many fields at once. Fihrist follows that idea: connect separate datasets and disciplines in one auditable index.

Fihrist was built for the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge, challenge Field Shift: Adapting Farms with NASA Data. The code is open source under the MIT license.

Beyond Earth

The same limits, further away

Every permanent settlement beyond Earth will have to farm in a closed loop. Water-stressed farms on Earth already live near those limits. The measures Fihrist reports (calories per cubic metre of water, nitrogen balance, people fed per hectare) are the same kinds used to evaluate bioregenerative life support. Fihrist shares those constraints; it does not design space habitats.

Fihrist supports, and does not replace, the judgment of farmers and agronomists.
